Last match Stevenage 2–1 Luton · 10 Sep · Lamex Stadium · still the most recent match played

Eighty per cent of the ball and two shots on target

The scoreline says a narrow away defeat. The shot profile says something worse, and more specific. Luton had ten shots and put two on target, for 0.83 expected goals — their lowest of the season by some distance, and less than half what they generated at home to Leyton Orient five days earlier. Per Luton Today's account they held over 70% of the ball in the first half and touched 80% at one stage in the second. That is not a side being kept out. That is a side circulating the ball in front of an opponent who was content to let them.

The number that matters is not the possession or even the xG total but the ratio underneath it: 0.083 expected goals per shot, the worst rate Luton have managed in any match this season. When they do create, they create decent chances — their season figure of 0.12 xG per shot ranks 7th in the division. At Stevenage they were taking whatever was available from wherever they happened to be. Eighty per cent of the ball and no idea what to do with it is a harder problem to fix than being pressed off the pitch, because it isn't a shape failure you can point at on video.

Both goals went through the goalkeeper

Stevenage scored twice from nine shots and 1.28 xG. Neither goal was a good chance by the model's reckoning, and both had Josh Keeley (GK) in them. Dan Kemp's opener on 23 minutes was struck from 22 yards and, per Luton Today, burst through Keeley's hands. The second on 70 came from a Ben Broggio cross that deflected off Keeley and struck Josh Magennis — who looked offside — before rebounding in. The beat also flagged a rash clearance on 28 minutes that Keeley misjudged as it bounced over him.

Mads Andersen (CB) got one back on 75, and that too was a deflection: a Kasey Palmer (AM) volley struck Andersen and wrongfooted the Stevenage goalkeeper. Three goals in the match, and on the available accounts none of them was cleanly struck past a set goalkeeper.

Luton Today's ratings, published on 11 Sep, put Keeley at 3 out of 10 and made the criticism more specific than "he was at fault": that he struggled with shots from distance, particularly to his right, and that the rush from goal was a poor decision rather than bad luck. A directional weakness named by someone who watched it is a different class of evidence from a season-long gap in a spreadsheet, and it is the kind of claim a club's own data would confirm or kill in an afternoon.

The rest of the card is worth noting for what it says about the team, not the goalkeeper. Andersen was named star of the match — with a 5. Hakeem Odoffin (CB) got 3.5, beaten repeatedly by Stevenage's wide players; Izzy Jones (LB) 4.5, lively early and unwilling to commit when the chances came. A beat writer who hands out a 5 as his highest mark is describing a flat night, not an unlucky one.

What the numbers cannot settle

This is the sixth game of evidence on the season's central question, and it is the first that points somewhere specific. Across six games Luton have conceded 13 on 6.6 xGA. On the night, the goalkeeper was directly implicated in both concessions. The temptation is to close the case.

Resist it, for a reason worth being precise about: the data cannot distinguish between a goalkeeper conceding more than he should and opponents striking the ball better than average. Expected goals measures the chance at the moment of the shot — position, angle, situation. It knows nothing about where the shot ended up. The metric that would settle this is post-shot expected goals, which weights a shot by where it crossed the line, and it is not in the free data. A 22-yard strike into a top corner and a 22-yard strike at the goalkeeper's midriff carry identical xG. Only one is a goalkeeping error.

So what we have is a large gap, six games long, and beat reporting that repeatedly puts Keeley at the centre of it. That is suggestive. It is not proof, and anyone presenting it as proof is over-reading a free dataset.

One thing did move this week, though. A directional observation — beaten to his right from distance — is falsifiable in a way the aggregate is not. If it is a real technical flaw, opponents will keep testing that side and it will show up again within a handful of matches. If it was one bad night described in hindsight, it will not. That is a cheaper test than waiting for the goals-minus-xGA gap to regress, and it is the thing to watch on Saturday rather than the scoreline.

The shape, and the bench

Wilshere made three changes again — the rotation rate the brief has been tracking holds. Joe Johnson took his first league start; Dan Gore (CM) and Callum Marshall (CF) returned. Nahki Wells (CF) and Jake Richards began on the bench. Kodua and Donley came off on 58 for Palmer and Richards; Marshall made way for Wells on 66. Luton's goal arrived nine minutes after the second of those changes, from the man introduced in the first.

The sequencing is the interesting part. The attack did not start generating until the bench emptied, and the manager who would normally be driving that from the touchline had been in the stands since the 24th minute. Wilshere was sent off for objecting to a non-call on Liam Walsh (CM) in the buildup to Kemp's goal — Powell's account, given on 11 Sep, is that Daniel Phillips had Walsh's shirt as he pressed him, that the whole ground expected the whistle, and that the referee, Andrew Humphries, disagreed and then dismissed the protest. Powell called both that and the Magennis offside blatant, and used the occasion to argue for VAR in League One, which tells you how the club is choosing to frame the week. How much a manager in the stand costs a team is unmeasurable, and worth flagging as unmeasurable rather than quietly folded into the analysis. But the game's shape did change after Luton finally changed it, later than they might have.

Next up Bradford City (h) · Sat 19 Sep · 15:00 UK

A side that has learned to win 1–0

Bradford arrive third, with twelve points from six and four wins, and a profile that is the precise inverse of Luton's. They concede 0.84 expected goals a game — the best defensive process in the division, 1st of 24. They allow 9.5 shots a game, second fewest, and they have faced thirteen shots on target in six matches — fewer than any other club in League One, a shade over two a game. Graham Alexander's side does not make saves because it does not need to.

They create very little in return. 1.12 xG a game ranks 17th; 11.3 shots a game ranks 18th; the chances they take are worth 0.099 each against a league norm of 0.104. They have scored six in six and the model says they should have scored 6.7, so there is no hidden finishing bonus either. Four of the six wins and defeats have been settled by a single goal, and their last two away days — 1–0 at Sheffield Wednesday, 1–0 at Notts County — followed the same script: concede territory, stay level to half-time, score once after it.

The Notts County win on 12 September is the template worth having in mind. Per Sky Sports' report, County had the better of a goalless first half, Callum Connolly cleared a shot off the line, and then George Pratt — a defender on loan from Blackburn — struck from twenty yards on 52 minutes for his first EFL goal. Bradford won 1–0 having generated 0.50 expected goals in the entire match. That is not a fluke result so much as the whole method: keep it at nil, take one long-range swing, defend the lead.

The watch list — four things to look for from kickoff

  1. Where Bradford shoot from, and which side of Keeley. Bradford take poor chances by design and score them from distance — Pratt's twenty-yarder at Notts County, and only four shots in the whole game at Plymouth. Luton Today's ratings after Stevenage named a specific weakness: long-range shots to Keeley's right. That is a falsifiable claim, and Saturday is the first test of it. If Bradford's first two or three efforts are from 20+ yards and one is to that side, watch the save, not the scoreline.
  2. Luton's shot-on-target count at the hour mark. This is the cleanest single measure of whether the Stevenage problem has been fixed. Bradford concede 2.2 on target a game, the fewest in the league; Luton average 4.5. If Luton have 65% of the ball and one or two on target by the 60th minute, it is Stevenage again — possession without penetration. Four or more and the plan is working regardless of the score.
  3. The corner count in the first half hour. Bradford win 7.0 corners a game, fourth in the division, and 6.3 a game on the road; Luton concede 4.0, the fourth fewest. One of those numbers gives. Since Bradford's open-play threat is genuinely poor, the set piece is their realistic route to a goal — so an early run of corners is the warning sign, and a first half in which they win one or two means Luton have taken their best weapon away.
  4. Wilshere's first substitution, and whether he is on the touchline to make it. Luton's season lives after half-time — nine of twelve goals scored, four of thirteen conceded — and at Stevenage the attack only came alive once the bench emptied on 58 and 66 minutes, with the manager in the stand. Bradford also concede late: three of their four goals against have come after the break. If Luton are level at 55 minutes and the changes come before the hour rather than after it, that is the manager backing his read. It is also the clearest thing on this list that is a coaching decision rather than a dice roll.

Where the game is likely to be decided

Can Luton's home creation get through the division's tightest defence? At Kenilworth Road, Luton generate 1.85 xG a game and concede 0.73. Bradford's away numbers are much thinner than their home ones — 0.67 xG for and 1.00 against in three away games, in which they were clearly out-chanced twice, dead level in the third, and still took six points from nine. Both sides' better trait meets the other's, and the three-game sample on Bradford's travels is too small to lean on hard.

Does the conceding problem exist at home? On the evidence, barely. Luton's goals-conceded-over-xGA gap is +5.6 in three away games and +0.8 in three at home, and at Kenilworth Road they have faced only nine shots on target all season. If Saturday is the low-event grind both profiles predict, the margin is one goal either way — which is exactly the kind of game the season's variance has been deciding, and exactly the kind of game Bradford have already won twice on the road.

The uncomfortable symmetry. Bradford's method requires the opponent to be wasteful with territory. Luton were wasteful with territory at Stevenage, against a side with a worse defence than this one. The thing that would break the pattern is not more possession — it is getting a goal before Bradford settle into a lead they know how to protect.

The matchup in numbers Luton's tendencies against Bradford's · team-level data through 12 Sep 2026

Where every team sits, and why these two are opposites

Each dot is a League One club, placed by how many chances it creates and how many it allows, per game. Luton sit in the creating-plenty half of the chart with a respectable defensive record. Bradford sit at the very top on suppression and in the bottom third on creation. Their game is a straight collision between Luton's willingness to generate and Bradford's refusal to allow.

Read the horizontal position with the sample in mind. Bradford's 1.12 xG a game is not thin by accident: they take 11.3 shots a match, eighteenth in the league, and the chances they take are ordinary ones, 0.099 xG per shot against a league norm of 0.104. They have scored six in six and the model says they should have scored 6.7. Nothing in the attacking column suggests a side that will overwhelm Luton. The threat, if it comes, comes from somewhere else, and the next chart says where.

The first half is where Luton's season has been lost

Nine of the thirteen goals Luton have conceded came before half-time. They have never led at the break: level three times, behind three times. After the interval the picture inverts — nine of their twelve goals scored, four conceded. Whatever the cause, and team-level data cannot say whether it is a slow start, an early press that fades, or opponents front-loading their plan, the pattern is six games deep and it is the single most Luton-specific fact in the dataset.

Now put Bradford's habits next to it. All six of Bradford's matches were level or a single goal apart at half-time, and four of the six were goalless at the break. Their first halves are low-event by design. Their goals arrive late — five of six in the second half — and so do the goals they concede, three of four. So the game's likeliest shape is a quiet first 45 followed by a contested second, and that shape is the one Luton have been good at. The condition is surviving the first half without the usual gift. If Bradford's tendency holds, they will not be pressing for one.

The dumbbells show the other thing worth knowing: the conceding gap is not evenly spread. It is Reading, Mansfield and Stevenage — all away — that produced it. At home, Luton have conceded three from 2.2 expected. Bradford's own dumbbells are short. They concede what they should, no more, which is what a well-organised defence looks like in this data.

Bradford's realistic route to a goal is a dead ball

Bradford win 7.0 corners a game, fourth in the division, and they do it away from home too — nine at Sheffield Wednesday, six at Plymouth, four at Notts County, 6.3 a game on the road. For a side that takes few shots and poor ones, that is the tell: they get to the byline and into the box without turning it into open-play chances, and they accumulate set pieces instead. Luton, for their part, concede four corners a game, fourth fewest. One of those two numbers gives on Saturday.

The Sunday page asked this run to check how many of Bradford's goals actually come from corners, and the answer is: fewer than the corner count implies. The free file cannot say directly — it is a shot-by-shot question — but the match reports can, and the one Bradford goal this run could trace to a report was not a set piece at all: the winner at Notts County was George Pratt striking from twenty yards in open play. The other five have not been traced, so this is one data point rather than a pattern. On it, the dead ball is Bradford's most likely route on the balance of volume, not a proven source of what they have actually scored. Treat the corner count as a pressure gauge on Saturday rather than as a scoring prediction — and note that a side winning seven corners a game while scoring one goal a game is, on one reading, a side failing to convert its best weapon.

The variance problem, seen across the league

Every club's goals conceded minus the chances they allowed. Luton are no longer the outlier at the foot of this chart — Bromley passed them on 12 September, conceding two from 1.1 expected in a 2–2 draw at Blackpool to reach +6.7 against Luton's +6.4. That is worth stating plainly because the last three editions of this page called Luton's gap the largest in the league, and as of this week it is the second largest. It is also the less interesting of the two facts. Bromley have allowed 48 shots on target to get there; Luton have allowed 23. Bromley are being beaten up. Luton are being beaten by very little.

Which is why the rate, not the total, is the number to carry: 57% of the on-target shots Luton have faced have gone in, against a league average of 37%. That is 24th of 24, and the next-worst club, Barnsley at 50%, is six and a half percentage points behind. Rates like that do not persist. Either the goalkeeping is the cause, in which case a change fixes it, or it is a run, in which case it fixes itself. Bradford sit comfortably the other side of the average: they allow few shots, poor ones, and concede about what those shots are worth.

The honest framing for Saturday: Luton's process is the fifth-best in League One and their results are twelfth. Bradford's process is ninth and their results are third. If the game is decided on the balance of chances, it favours the home side. It has not been decided that way for Luton very often this season, and it has not been decided that way against Bradford at all — they were out-chanced in two of three away games, level in the third, and won two of them.

Player watch standing interests

Jordan Clark CM · the club's most important player

Closer, and the calendar has moved in his favour — but no closer than he was eight days ago, because nothing has been published since. Non-contact training for nine or ten days as of 9 Sep, progressed from running to ball work, waiting on a protective mask before contact resumes. Wilshere said he did not expect it to be much longer and described the absence in terms of energy and leadership rather than goals — the right frame, and the one to test on his return. With Huddersfield and Doncaster postponed, the league games he stands to miss between now and mid-October number two: Bradford on Saturday and Wycombe on 10 Oct. A fortnight ago that same recovery would have cost him four.

Expect him to miss Saturday. A mask still unfitted on 9 Sep, and no announcement in the eight days since, is not the shape of a player who trains in contact this week — and Thursday's press conference, which lands after this page is compiled, is where a return would be trailed. If he does start, that is genuine news and the plan for the game changes; treat the watch list here as written for a Luton side without him.

The Stevenage performance sharpens what to watch for. Luton did not lack the ball; they lacked anyone to do something decisive with it in the final third. Clark's twelve league goals last season came from a midfielder arriving rather than a forward finishing. If the attack's shape changes on his return — more penetration, fewer sideways circuits — that is the evidence. If Luton simply score more from the same flat possession, it was finishing variance all along.

Josh Keeley GK · the defensive-variance question

The most consequential ninety minutes of his season so far, and not in a good way. Beaten from 22 yards through his hands for the opener, deflected a cross onto Magennis for the second, and misjudged a clearance on 28 minutes. Luton Today marked him 3 out of 10, the lowest on the card, and put the criticism on long-range shooting to his right specifically. Six games in, the side has conceded 13 on 6.6 expected.

What can honestly be said: the gap is real, it is the second-largest in the division on the raw total and comfortably the most extreme once you account for how few chances Luton actually concede, and the beat now describes a particular weakness rather than a general one. What cannot be said from this data is that he is the cause, because the free dataset has no post-shot xG and therefore cannot separate poor goalkeeping from well-struck shots. The named weakness is the useful part — it predicts something.

Saturday is an unusually clean test of it. Bradford's chances are ordinary ones — 0.099 expected goals per shot against a league norm of 0.104 — which is the profile of a side that shoots from where it can rather than where it wants, and their most recent goal was a twenty-yard strike from a defender. A side that scores from distance, against a goalkeeper the beat says is vulnerable from distance to his right. If the flaw is real, this is the fixture that exposes it; if Luton keep a clean sheet against opponents who shoot from exactly there, that is meaningful evidence the other way. Either result tells you something, which is rarer than it sounds. The name, checked again this week against the club's own site and the ratings card, is Josh.

Callum Marshall / Nahki Wells CF · the striker choice, tracked as a pair

Marshall started at Stevenage and came off on 66 for Wells — the same pattern as the rotation elsewhere in the side, and still no sign of the two being trusted on the pitch together. Neither had much to work with: two shots on target between the whole team.

The pairing question stays open because the situation that would force it — chasing a game, needing a goal — arrived at Stevenage and Wilshere still went one-for-one rather than putting both on. That is a choice, not an accident.

Saturday is the likeliest occasion yet to force it. Bradford defend a lead better than anyone in the division and concede two shots on target a game; if Luton are behind or level at 65 minutes, the one-striker-at-a-time policy has to survive its hardest test of the season. Wells is the top scorer and came on at Stevenage; Marshall has started the last two. Which of them starts is the smaller question. Whether both finish is the one that tells you what Wilshere thinks his squad is for.

Talking points for Megan

  1. The goalkeeper case is the strongest it has been and still isn't proven. Thirteen conceded on 6.6 expected is the second-biggest gap in League One — Bromley overtook Luton on 12 September — but Bromley got there by conceding 48 shots on target and Luton by conceding 23, which is the more damning of the two routes. At Stevenage he was in both goals. But expected goals stops at the moment of the shot — it cannot tell a save that should have been made from a shot that could not have been saved. Anyone who says the numbers prove it is a goalkeeping problem is claiming more than the numbers can carry. Anyone who says it is pure luck is ignoring that it has happened six times.
  2. Luton are the best team in the bottom half of this table. Fifth-best xG difference in the division, twelfth on points, negative goal difference — and they fell four places on a weekend they did not play, which is its own comment on how little six games settles. Either the underlying numbers are about to start paying — in which case this is a promotion side having a bad month — or there is something the model cannot see, and the something is standing between the posts.
  3. The first half is the whole argument. Nine of thirteen conceded before the break, nine of twelve scored after it. Is that a fitness curve, a game-plan that starts too passively, or three bad away days in a row? Bradford's first halves are usually goalless, which either neutralises Luton's weakness or removes their excuse.
  4. The away problem is not a defending problem. Luton concede only moderately worse chances away than at home — 1.47 xGA against 0.73. The difference is that away, those chances go in: ten goals from 4.4 expected in three matches, against three from 2.2 at home. Whatever is wrong on the road, it is not the shape of the defence.
  5. Eighty per cent of the ball is not a compliment. Stevenage were happy to concede possession and Luton produced 0.83 xG from it. Bradford suppress chances better than anyone in the league — thirteen shots on target faced in six games, fewest in the division — and win corners at the fourth-highest rate. Expect them to invite exactly the same thing at Kenilworth Road.
  6. The case against Bradford, for the sake of the argument. They are third with the ninth-best process, they have been level or worse on chances in all three away games and took six points from them anyway, and they have scored six goals in six matches. A side whose method is "stay level, score once, hold on" is one bad afternoon from looking very ordinary, and the free numbers say the afternoon is overdue. The counter — and Megan will make it — is that four clean sheets in six and thirteen shots on target conceded is not luck, it is a defensive structure, and structures travel better than finishing does. Both of those are defensible from the same dataset, which is the honest state of it.
  7. The season just went on hold, and that cuts both ways. Two postponed league fixtures mean one league match in twenty-one days. Good: Clark gets back for a schedule that has barely started, and a squad rotating three a game gets a proper block of training instead. Bad: if Saturday goes badly, Luton sit on it for three weeks, and a six-game sample that everyone is already over-reading becomes a seven-game sample nobody can add to. The argument worth having is whether a break helps a side whose problem is variance — it does not fix variance, but it does stop it compounding.
  8. Analytical observations only. No lines, no recommendations.

Beyond free data what the club's own feed would add

Everything above is built from one free file: a line per match, with each side's goals, shots and expected goals. It can say how much Luton create and concede. It cannot say where from, who, or how the goalkeeper did once the shot was struck.

The picture on the right is what the shot-level view looks like, drawn from StatsBomb's free sample of an older Premier League season. Every dot is a shot, sized by how good a chance it was, coloured if it went in. With Luton's own StatsBomb feed — the one the club's analysts already use — this page could show the same for every Luton match and every opponent, plus the post-shot xG that would settle the Keeley question one way or the other. Distance covered and sprint data would come from the club's Catapult vests, not from any data vendor.

Prototype work lives in a separate folder, hatters-scout, and runs on the same code that would consume the club's data.
