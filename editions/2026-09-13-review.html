Last match Stevenage 2–1 Luton · 10 Sep · Lamex Stadium

Eighty per cent of the ball and two shots on target

The scoreline says a narrow away defeat. The shot profile says something worse, and more specific. Luton had ten shots and put two on target, for 0.83 expected goals — their lowest of the season by some distance, and less than half what they generated at home to Leyton Orient five days earlier. Per Luton Today's account they held over 70% of the ball in the first half and touched 80% at one stage in the second. That is not a side being kept out. That is a side circulating the ball in front of an opponent who was content to let them.

The number that matters is not the possession or even the xG total but the ratio underneath it: 0.083 expected goals per shot, the worst rate Luton have managed in any match this season. When they do create, they create decent chances — their season figure of 0.12 xG per shot ranks 7th in the division. At Stevenage they were taking whatever was available from wherever they happened to be. Eighty per cent of the ball and no idea what to do with it is a harder problem to fix than being pressed off the pitch, because it isn't a shape failure you can point at on video.

Both goals went through the goalkeeper

Stevenage scored twice from nine shots and 1.28 xG. Neither goal was a good chance by the model's reckoning, and both had Josh Keeley (GK) in them. Dan Kemp's opener on 23 minutes was struck from 22 yards and, per Luton Today, burst through Keeley's hands. The second on 70 came from a Ben Broggio cross that deflected off Keeley and struck Josh Magennis — who looked offside — before rebounding in. The beat also flagged a rash clearance on 28 minutes that Keeley misjudged as it bounced over him.

Mads Andersen (CB) got one back on 75, and that too was a deflection: a Kasey Palmer (AM) volley struck Andersen and wrongfooted the Stevenage goalkeeper. Three goals in the match, and on the available accounts none of them was cleanly struck past a set goalkeeper.

Luton Today's ratings, published on 11 Sep, put Keeley at 3 out of 10 and made the criticism more specific than "he was at fault": that he struggled with shots from distance, particularly to his right, and that the rush from goal was a poor decision rather than bad luck. A directional weakness named by someone who watched it is a different class of evidence from a season-long gap in a spreadsheet, and it is the kind of claim a club's own data would confirm or kill in an afternoon.

The rest of the card is worth noting for what it says about the team, not the goalkeeper. Andersen was named star of the match — with a 5. Hakeem Odoffin (CB) got 3.5, beaten repeatedly by Stevenage's wide players; Izzy Jones (LB) 4.5, lively early and unwilling to commit when the chances came. A beat writer who hands out a 5 as his highest mark is describing a flat night, not an unlucky one.

What the numbers cannot settle

This is the sixth game of evidence on the season's central question, and it is the first that points somewhere specific. Across six games Luton have conceded 13 on 6.6 xGA. On the night, the goalkeeper was directly implicated in both concessions. The temptation is to close the case.

Resist it, for a reason worth being precise about: the data cannot distinguish between a goalkeeper conceding more than he should and opponents striking the ball better than average. Expected goals measures the chance at the moment of the shot — position, angle, situation. It knows nothing about where the shot ended up. The metric that would settle this is post-shot expected goals, which weights a shot by where it crossed the line, and it is not in the free data. A 22-yard strike into a top corner and a 22-yard strike at the goalkeeper's midriff carry identical xG. Only one is a goalkeeping error.

So what we have is a large gap, six games long, and beat reporting that repeatedly puts Keeley at the centre of it. That is suggestive. It is not proof, and anyone presenting it as proof is over-reading a free dataset.

One thing did move this week, though. A directional observation — beaten to his right from distance — is falsifiable in a way the aggregate is not. If it is a real technical flaw, opponents will keep testing that side and it will show up again within a handful of matches. If it was one bad night described in hindsight, it will not. That is a cheaper test than waiting for the goals-minus-xGA gap to regress, and it is the thing to watch on Saturday rather than the scoreline.

The shape, and the bench

Wilshere made three changes again — the rotation rate the brief has been tracking holds. Joe Johnson took his first league start; Dan Gore (CM) and Callum Marshall (CF) returned. Nahki Wells (CF) and Jake Richards began on the bench. Kodua and Donley came off on 58 for Palmer and Richards; Marshall made way for Wells on 66. Luton's goal arrived nine minutes after the second of those changes, from the man introduced in the first.

The sequencing is the interesting part. The attack did not start generating until the bench emptied, and the manager who would normally be driving that from the touchline had been in the stands since the 24th minute. Wilshere was sent off for objecting to a non-call on Liam Walsh (CM) in the buildup to Kemp's goal — Powell's account, given on 11 Sep, is that Daniel Phillips had Walsh's shirt as he pressed him, that the whole ground expected the whistle, and that the referee, Andrew Humphries, disagreed and then dismissed the protest. Powell called both that and the Magennis offside blatant, and used the occasion to argue for VAR in League One, which tells you how the club is choosing to frame the week. How much a manager in the stand costs a team is unmeasurable, and worth flagging as unmeasurable rather than quietly folded into the analysis. But the game's shape did change after Luton finally changed it, later than they might have.

Next up Bradford City (h) · Sat 19 Sep · 15:00 UK

Bradford arrive 5th, with nine points from five, and a profile that is the precise inverse of Luton's. They concede 0.91 expected goals a game — the best defensive process in the division, ranked 1st of 24. They also create very little: 1.24 xG a game ranks 16th, and they have scored five goals in five matches while underperforming their own xG by 1.2. Three of their wins have been of the 1–0, 2–0 variety. This is a side that wins by not letting anything happen.

The postponements load the fixture. With no league game on 26 Sep or 3 Oct, Saturday is Luton's only league match in a twenty-one-day stretch, and whatever it produces will sit on the table until 10 October. Two things decide it, and the Thursday run will take them apart properly:

One: can Luton's home creation get through the division's tightest defence? At Kenilworth Road, Luton generate 1.85 xG a game and concede 0.73. Bradford's away numbers are much thinner than their home ones — 0.76 xG for and 1.26 against in two away games. Both sides' better trait meets the other's, and the two-game sample on Bradford's travels is far too small to lean on.

Two: does the conceding problem exist at home? On the evidence, barely. Luton's goals-conceded-over-xGA gap is +5.6 in three away games and +0.8 in three at home. If the Bradford game is the low-event grind their profile predicts, the margin is one goal either way — which is exactly the kind of game the season's variance has been deciding.

The matchup in numbers Luton's tendencies against Bradford's · team-level data through 10 Sep 2026

Where every team sits, and why these two are opposites

Each dot is a League One club, placed by how many chances it creates and how many it allows, per game. Luton sit in the creating-plenty half of the chart with a respectable defensive record. Bradford sit at the very top on suppression and in the bottom third on creation. Their game is a straight collision between Luton's willingness to generate and Bradford's refusal to allow.

Read the horizontal position with the sample in mind. Bradford's 1.24 xG a game is not thin by accident: they take twelve shots a match, sixteenth in the league, and the chances they take are ordinary ones, 0.10 xG per shot against a league norm of 0.11. They have scored five in five and the model says they should have scored six. Nothing in the attacking column suggests a side that will overwhelm Luton. The threat, if it comes, comes from somewhere else, and the next chart says where.

The first half is where Luton's season has been lost

Nine of the thirteen goals Luton have conceded came before half-time. They have never led at the break: level three times, behind three times. After the interval the picture inverts — nine of their twelve goals scored, four conceded. Whatever the cause, and team-level data cannot say whether it is a slow start, an early press that fades, or opponents front-loading their plan, the pattern is six games deep and it is the single most Luton-specific fact in the dataset.

Now put Bradford's habits next to it. Four of Bradford's five matches were level or a single goal apart at half-time; three were goalless. Bradford's first halves are low-event by design. Their goals arrive late — four of five in the second half — and so do the goals they concede, three of four. So the game's likeliest shape is a quiet first 45 followed by a contested second, and that shape is the one Luton have been good at. The condition is surviving the first half without the usual gift. If Bradford's tendency holds, they will not be pressing for one.

The dumbbells show the other thing worth knowing: the conceding gap is not evenly spread. It is Reading, Mansfield and Stevenage — all away — that produced it. At home, Luton have conceded three from 2.2 expected. Bradford's own dumbbells are short. They concede what they should, no more, which is what a well-organised defence looks like in this data.

Bradford's realistic route to a goal is a dead ball

Bradford win 7.6 corners a game, second in the division, and they do it away from home too — nine at Sheffield Wednesday, six at Plymouth. For a side that takes few shots and poor ones, that is the tell: they get to the byline and into the box without turning it into open-play chances, and they accumulate set pieces instead. Luton, for their part, concede four corners a game, fourth fewest. One of those two numbers gives on Saturday.

What this data cannot say is how many of Bradford's five goals actually came from corners. That is a shot-by-shot question and the free file does not carry it. The Thursday run should answer it from the match reports before anyone builds a plan around it.

The variance problem, seen across the league

Every club's goals conceded minus the chances they allowed. Luton are at the foot of it and nobody is close: 57% of on-target shots against them have gone in, against a league average of 37%. Rates like that do not persist. Either the goalkeeping is the cause, in which case a change fixes it, or it is a run, in which case it fixes itself. Bradford sit in the comfortable middle: they allow poor shots and concede about what those shots are worth.

The honest framing for Saturday: Luton's process is the fifth-best in League One and their results are eighth. Bradford's process is seventh and their results are fifth. If the game is decided on the balance of chances, it favours the home side. It has not been decided that way for Luton very often this season.

Shape: not a run of Saturdays at all. Luton announced on 7 Sep that Huddersfield away and Doncaster at home are postponed for international call-ups — the club has not said which players. Nine days separate Stevenage from Bradford, the longest rest of the season so far and Wilshere's first real training week. After Bradford, the league programme stops: an under-21 Trophy tie on the 22nd, nothing on the 26th, nothing on 3 Oct, a Trophy tie at Colchester on the 6th, and no competitive league football again until Wycombe away on 10 Oct — twenty-one days after the Bradford game. No rearranged dates have been published.

Put the two gaps end to end and the shape is starker than either half suggests. Between the Stevenage defeat on 10 September and the Wycombe game on 10 October — a calendar month — Luton play one league match. Six games have been played and the seventh is the only one the division will see from them until the middle of October. That is an unusual thing to happen to a season in progress, and it is why the sample problem on this page gets worse before it gets better.

That matters more than a fixture note usually does. It gives Jordan Clark (CM) three further weeks to be fit for a league match rather than three further games to miss, and it means Saturday is the only league evidence anyone gets on the conceding question for three weeks. Whatever happens at Kenilworth Road will be over-read, by this page as much as anyone. Kickoff times for non-televised Saturdays are the standard 15:00 and are subject to Sky moving them.

Player watch standing interests

Jordan Clark CM · the club's most important player

Closer, and the calendar has just moved in his favour. Non-contact training for nine or ten days as of 9 Sep, progressed from running to ball work, and waiting on a protective mask before contact resumes. Wilshere said he did not expect it to be much longer and described the absence in terms of energy and leadership rather than goals — which is the right frame, and the one to test on his return. With Huddersfield and Doncaster postponed, the league games he stands to miss between now and mid-October number two: Bradford on Saturday and Wycombe on 10 Oct. A fortnight ago that same recovery would have cost him four.

The Stevenage performance sharpens what to watch for. Luton did not lack the ball; they lacked anyone to do something decisive with it in the final third. Clark's twelve league goals last season came from a midfielder arriving rather than a forward finishing. If the attack's shape changes on his return — more penetration, fewer sideways circuits — that is the evidence. If Luton simply score more from the same flat possession, it was finishing variance all along.

Josh Keeley GK · the defensive-variance question

The most consequential ninety minutes of his season so far, and not in a good way. Beaten from 22 yards through his hands for the opener, deflected a cross onto Magennis for the second, and misjudged a clearance on 28 minutes. Luton Today marked him 3 out of 10, the lowest on the card, and put the criticism on long-range shooting to his right specifically. Six games in, the side has conceded 13 on 6.6 expected.

What can honestly be said: the gap is real, it is the largest in the division, and the beat now describes a particular weakness rather than a general one. What cannot be said from this data is that he is the cause, because the free dataset has no post-shot xG and therefore cannot separate poor goalkeeping from well-struck shots. The named weakness is the useful part — it predicts something. Bradford's chances are ordinary ones — 0.10 expected goals per shot against a league norm of 0.11 — which is the profile of a side that shoots from where it can rather than where it wants. Whether they go looking for that right-hand side is worth watching from the first ten minutes. The name, checked again this week against the club's own site and the ratings card, is Josh.

Callum Marshall / Nahki Wells CF · the striker choice, tracked as a pair

Marshall started at Stevenage and came off on 66 for Wells — the same pattern as the rotation elsewhere in the side, and still no sign of the two being trusted on the pitch together. Neither had much to work with: two shots on target between the whole team.

The pairing question stays open because the situation that would force it — chasing a game, needing a goal — arrived at Stevenage and Wilshere still went one-for-one rather than putting both on. That is a choice, not an accident, and worth watching again at home to Bradford, where Luton will have most of the ball against a side that concedes less than anyone.

Talking points for Megan

  1. The goalkeeper case is the strongest it has been and still isn't proven. Thirteen conceded on 6.6 expected is the biggest gap in League One, and at Stevenage he was in both goals. But expected goals stops at the moment of the shot — it cannot tell a save that should have been made from a shot that could not have been saved. Anyone who says the numbers prove it is a goalkeeping problem is claiming more than the numbers can carry. Anyone who says it is pure luck is ignoring that it has happened six times.
  2. Luton are the best team in the bottom half of this table. Fifth-best xG difference in the division, eighth on points, negative goal difference. Either the underlying numbers are about to start paying — in which case this is a promotion side having a bad month — or there is something the model cannot see, and the something is standing between the posts.
  3. The first half is the whole argument. Nine of thirteen conceded before the break, nine of twelve scored after it. Is that a fitness curve, a game-plan that starts too passively, or three bad away days in a row? Bradford's first halves are usually goalless, which either neutralises Luton's weakness or removes their excuse.
  4. The away problem is not a defending problem. Luton concede only moderately worse chances away than at home — 1.47 xGA against 0.73. The difference is that away, those chances go in: ten goals from 4.4 expected in three matches, against three from 2.2 at home. Whatever is wrong on the road, it is not the shape of the defence.
  5. Eighty per cent of the ball is not a compliment. Stevenage were happy to concede possession and Luton produced 0.83 xG from it. Bradford suppress chances better than anyone in the league and win corners at the second-highest rate. Expect them to invite exactly the same thing at Kenilworth Road, and to make the set pieces count at the other end.
  6. The season just went on hold, and that cuts both ways. Two postponed league fixtures mean one league match in twenty-one days. Good: Clark gets back for a schedule that has barely started, and a squad rotating three a game gets a proper block of training instead. Bad: if Saturday goes badly, Luton sit on it for three weeks, and a six-game sample that everyone is already over-reading becomes a seven-game sample nobody can add to. The argument worth having is whether a break helps a side whose problem is variance — it does not fix variance, but it does stop it compounding.
  7. Analytical observations only. No lines, no recommendations.

Beyond free data what the club's own feed would add

Everything above is built from one free file: a line per match, with each side's goals, shots and expected goals. It can say how much Luton create and concede. It cannot say where from, who, or how the goalkeeper did once the shot was struck.

The picture on the right is what the shot-level view looks like, drawn from StatsBomb's free sample of an older Premier League season. Every dot is a shot, sized by how good a chance it was, coloured if it went in. With Luton's own StatsBomb feed — the one the club's analysts already use — this page could show the same for every Luton match and every opponent, plus the post-shot xG that would settle the Keeley question one way or the other. Distance covered and sprint data would come from the club's Catapult vests, not from any data vendor.

Prototype work lives in a separate folder, hatters-scout, and runs on the same code that would consume the club's data.
